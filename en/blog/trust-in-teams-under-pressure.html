Trust in Teams Is a Pattern, Not a Value: What Leaders Teach Under Pressure

Esther Michele Kazir · Nov 18, 2026 · לקריאה בעברית

Trust is usually described as a value.

In practice, people experience it as a pattern.

Do commitments hold when pressure rises?
Are difficult decisions explained, or simply announced?
What happens to the person who raises a risk, challenges an assumption, or admits a mistake?

Each of those moments teaches the team what is safe to say.

What the research suggests

Research on social decision making, well summarized in a 2011 review by Rilling and Sanfey, shows that people track how others behave over repeated interactions and adjust how much they cooperate accordingly. Trust in this sense is learned from experience. It is not declared.

What this means in practice

Under pressure, leaders do not only make decisions. They also teach the system what to expect from power.

A team can believe you are trustworthy in principle and still stay quiet when it counts, because they cannot predict how you will react when the answer is inconvenient.

A question for this week

Think of the last time someone brought you bad news.

What did your reaction teach them about bringing you the next one?

Source: Rilling, J. K., & Sanfey, A. G. (2011). The neuroscience of social decision-making. Annual Review of Psychology, 62, 23-48. doi.org/10.1146/annurev.psych.121208.131647
